Abstract

This chapter develops the three extensions announced at the end of the chapter “Self-negation and its stable forms”. First, it compares families of involutions and their domains. Linear involutions fix zero; affine involutions on the whole space have fixed points, and some exclude zero. For an orthogonal involution , the map , extended by continuity to the one-point compactification, exchanges zero and infinity and fixes the unit sphere of the eigenspace of . Within these two families, equivariance under the automorphisms of , or together with requirement (N2) selects four maps; the two inversive ones are the antipodal negation, with no fixed point, and the self-negation , whose fixed points form . This is not a classification of all negations, and it is the continuity of the extension, not magnitude reversal alone, that excludes zero. Second, in low-dimensional Clifford algebras the square roots of form more varied sets: the empty set, spheres, the product , a two-sheeted hyperboloid, and unions of components of different dimensions. The complex structures of , , form a manifold of dimension with two components; in dimension two, passing to Möbius transformations identifies with . Third, in a linear open system with positive-definite dissipation, a nonzero periodic solution requires strictly positive mean input power, equal to the dissipated power; without dissipation, oscillations persist without input. The recurrence proved concerns a finite-dimensional orthogonal group. No cosmological conclusion and no theory of consciousness follow from these results.

1Introduction

The previous chapter established that self-negation , defined on the nonzero elements of , has as fixed points exactly the square roots of , which form the sphere of unit pure imaginaries. It showed that this result depends on the choice of negation, since the additive negation fixes zero. Finally, it related the stable forms to conservative evolutions, without beginning or end. Three questions were left open; this chapter addresses them.

The first concerns the choice of negation. Which families of involutions satisfy requirements (N1) of involutivity and (N2) of no nonzero real fixed point? Which of them include zero in their domain, and what then becomes of their fixed points? The second concerns the algebraic substrate. What becomes of the stable forms when one leaves the division algebras for Clifford algebras? The third concerns the physics of the substrate. Under what hypotheses does the persistence of a function in an open system require input and dissipation? Strictly positive dissipation is not presupposed for every open system, and the dynamical results do not follow from the algebraic fixed points alone.

The notation, axioms A1 to A3 and the badge legend are those of the previous chapter. Internal references of the form "I.4.1" point to the statements of its version 2.0. "Stable form" there means fixed point, with no implied attraction; A2 identifies zero with nothingness by stipulation, and A3 is only a necessary condition of representation, without converse.

4Reading: the two horns of the dilemma in a single algebra

Modeled The dilemma of the previous chapter set a model without fixed points, the Boolean model, against a model whose fixed points are imaginary. Theorem 3.5 shows that these two horns do not require a change of framework. In one and the same compactified algebra, with the same requirement of magnitude reversal and the same compatibility with the structure, the antipodal negation has no fixed point and the self-negation has a sphere of them. Neither fixes represented nothingness.

The same theorem locates the metaphysical commitment, within the families studied. Requirements (N1) and (N2) leave the four maps open. The two linear maps fix zero: under A2, they make nothingness a self-exclusion. The two inversive maps exclude it. Requirement (N3) of magnitude reversal is nevertheless not sufficient by itself: the map defined by and for is an involution of that satisfies (N1), (N2) and (N3) away from zero, and that fixes zero; it is discontinuous at the origin. It is the joint requirement of magnitude reversal and continuity of the extension to the compactification that forces . This twofold choice, conceiving negation as a continuous inversion rather than as an opposition, is not proved by the model; it is the thesis's own content.

Established Ionic substrate. The Hodgkin–Huxley model (1952) describes membrane electrical dynamics by ionic currents, sodium and potassium, and variable conductances. The reversal potentials are constant parameters in it; the metabolic maintenance of concentration gradients by ion pumps is not represented in its usual equations. Physiologically, these gradients are maintained at the cost of energy consumption. Modeled The programme of substrate-constrained functionalism takes ionic flows and endogenous electromagnetic fields to be the actual substrate of consciousness; this hypothesis calls for definitions, experiments and model comparisons. Theorem 7.5, which concerns linear systems, does not apply directly to the nonlinear Hodgkin–Huxley equations; a biological energy balance of its own would have to be built and calibrated.

Modeled The tension between the evolution without beginning or end of the conservative model and the arrow of time of the open models is not a contradiction but a difference in level of description. The global model can be conservative, reversible and recurrent; a part that sustains a function exhibits local irreversibility, paid for by a flux. A flow defined for every instant does not, for all that, establish that the universe has neither beginning nor end. Recurrence concerns the state of the model: a return near the initial state restores a function only if that function is defined and robust at that proximity. Corollary 9.2 therefore proves neither the indefinite restoration of every function nor the infinite dimension of the universe; the statistical treatment of initial states, correlations and time scales remains indispensable for discussing physical irreversibility.

Contested These remarks connect with Bertalanffy's theory of open systems (1950) and with Prigogine's thermodynamics of dissipative structures (Nobel lecture, 1977), according to which living structures maintain themselves far from equilibrium through exchanges of matter and energy. Their cosmological transposition requires additional hypotheses about the dynamics, the state space and the relevant measures; it lies outside the theorems of this chapter (see section I.8.6).

10Critical discussion and limitations

10.1What is established

The theorems of parts I and II are proved mathematical results, with the exception of the transitivity of on , the general framework of Clifford algebras and their classification, which are cited. The theorems of part III concern linear systems, or the van der Pol oscillator, whose limit-cycle existence is cited. The Hodgkin–Huxley model and Landauer's bound are published results, used here by way of illustration.

10.2What remains stipulated Normative

Part I shows that the exclusion of zero depends on the family of operators, the domain and the continuity imposed on the extension; requirement (N3) alone does not decide it, nor do (N1) and (N2). The model does not justify these choices; it isolates their scope. Part II shows that extending A2 to non-division algebras requires an additional decision about the status of noninvertible elements. Part III establishes conditional balances; identifying a mental function with a dynamical regime of an open system belongs to the research programme, not to the model.

10.3Limitations of the models

The open systems studied are linear, with the exception of the van der Pol oscillator. Real biological systems are nonlinear, high-dimensional and stochastic. The recurrence of theorem 9.1 is exact, but no universal return time follows from the dimension: if , every instant is a return, and oscillators with commensurable frequencies share a short period whatever their number. Return times depend on the frequencies, their arithmetic relations and the required precision; they can be very long. The numerical check in the appendix shows a return within a finite window; arbitrarily late returns are established by the proof. No empirical prediction specific to the proposed semantics is formulated.

11Conclusion

The three extensions lead to three results. First, every linear involution fixes zero, and the inversive negations , extended by continuity to the compactification, exclude it by exchanging it with infinity. Within these two families, equivariance and requirement (N2) select four maps, of which the two inversive ones realize the two horns of the dilemma of self-exclusion. The metaphysical commitment of the thesis is concentrated in the choice of an inversive and continuous negation, rather than an opposition; neither (N3) alone nor this result proves the impossibility of nothingness.

Second, in Clifford algebras, the stable forms of self-negation are the square roots of , which form sets more varied than spheres: the empty set, products of spheres, a two-sheeted hyperboloid, components of different dimensions, with the connectedness of proved. The complex structures of , , form a manifold of dimension with two components; for , the real Möbius involutions are its quotient by , identified with the hyperbolic plane.

Third, in a linear open system with positive-definite dissipation, a nonzero periodic persistence requires a strictly positive mean input, equal to the dissipation and bounded below by a constant that depends on the modeled substrate; without dissipation, oscillations persist without input. The recurrence of the finite-dimensional orthogonal model limits certain exact dissipative descriptions. None of these results establishes a cosmological thesis or a theory of consciousness; they articulate, within a single formal framework, the conservation of the global model and the dissipative persistence of its parts.

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import expm, null_space
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

rng = np.random.default_rng(20261030)

# ---------- I. Inversive negations f_L(x) = L x / |x|^2 on R^n (n = 2, 4, 8) ----------
def ortho_involution(n, k):
    """Involutive isometry L of R^n whose +1 eigenspace (dim k) does not contain e0 = 1."""
    while True:
        Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(rng.normal(size=(n, n)))
        Vp = Q[:, :k]
        L = 2 * Vp @ Vp.T - np.eye(n)
        if k == 0 or np.linalg.norm(Vp.T @ np.eye(n)[0]) < 0.99:  # 1 outside V+
            return L, Vp
for n in (2, 4, 8):
    for k in range(0, n):
        L, Vp = ortho_involution(n, k)
        f = lambda x: L @ x / np.dot(x, x)
        for _ in range(20):
            x = rng.normal(size=n)
            assert np.allclose(f(f(x)), x)                                   # N1
            a = rng.normal()
            assert not np.isclose(f(a * np.eye(n)[0]), a * np.eye(n)[0]).all()  # N2 on the real axis
        if k > 0:
            y = Vp @ rng.normal(size=k); y /= np.linalg.norm(y)
            assert np.allclose(f(y), y)                                      # S(V+) fixed
        x = rng.normal(size=n)
        assert not np.allclose(f(x), x)                                      # generic point not fixed
# alpha = -iota: no fixed point; nu = sigma o iota: S^{n-2}
for n in (2, 4, 8):
    alpha = lambda x: -x / np.dot(x, x)
    sig = np.diag([-1.0] + [1.0] * (n - 1))
    nu = lambda x: sig @ x / np.dot(x, x)
    for _ in range(50):
        x = rng.normal(size=n) * rng.uniform(0.1, 3)
        assert np.linalg.norm(alpha(x) - x) > 1e-9
        assert np.isclose(np.linalg.norm(nu(x)), 1 / np.linalg.norm(x))  # 0 <-> infinity
# Real involutive Möbius maps: M = [[a,b],[c,-a]], det = 1  =>  M^2 = -I, nonreal conjugate fixed points
for _ in range(50):
    a, u = rng.normal(size=2); w = np.sqrt(1 + a**2 + u**2) * rng.choice([-1, 1])
    b, c = w + u, u - w
    M = np.array([[a, b], [c, -a]])
    assert np.isclose(np.linalg.det(M), 1) and np.allclose(M @ M, -np.eye(2))
    roots = np.roots([c, -2 * a, -b])                                   # c z^2 - 2 a z - b = 0
    assert np.all(np.abs(roots.imag) > 1e-12) and np.isclose(roots[0], np.conj(roots[1]))

# ---------- II. Clifford algebras ----------
I2 = np.eye(2)
qi = np.array([[1j, 0], [0, -1j]]); qj = np.array([[0, 1], [-1, 0]], dtype=complex); qk = np.array([[0, 1j], [1j, 0]])
Z = np.zeros((2, 2))
ds = lambda A, B: np.block([[A, Z], [Z, B]])
E = [ds(q, -q) for q in (qi, qj, qk)]                                    # Cl(0,3) -> H + H
for p in range(3):
    assert np.allclose(E[p] @ E[p], -np.eye(4))
    for q in range(p + 1, 3):
        assert np.allclose(E[p] @ E[q], -E[q] @ E[p])
om = E[0] @ E[1] @ E[2]
assert np.allclose(om, ds(-I2, I2)) and np.allclose(om @ om, np.eye(4))
for p in range(3):
    assert np.allclose(om @ E[p], E[p] @ om)
basis = [np.eye(4)] + E + [E[0] @ E[1], E[0] @ E[2], E[1] @ E[2], om]
vec = lambda M: np.r_[M.real.ravel(), M.imag.ravel()]
assert np.linalg.matrix_rank(np.array([vec(B) for B in basis])) == 8     # isomorphism (dimension 8)
# Cl(1,1) -> M2(R)
e1 = np.array([[1., 0], [0, -1]]); e2 = np.array([[0., 1], [-1, 0]])
assert np.allclose(e1 @ e1, I2) and np.allclose(e2 @ e2, -I2) and np.allclose(e1 @ e2, -e2 @ e1)
assert np.linalg.matrix_rank(np.array([m.ravel() for m in (I2, e1, e2, e1 @ e2)])) == 4
# Cl(3,0) -> M2(C) via the Pauli matrices; pseudoscalar = i I
sx = np.array([[0, 1], [1, 0]], dtype=complex); sy = np.array([[0, -1j], [1j, 0]]); sz = np.diag([1, -1]).astype(complex)
assert np.allclose(sx @ sy @ sz, 1j * I2)
assert np.linalg.matrix_rank(np.array([vec(B) for B in (I2, sx, sy, sz, sx@sy, sx@sz, sy@sz, sx@sy@sz)])) == 8
# Square roots of -1 in M2(C): J = i S, S^2 = I, S = [[a,b],[c,-a]], a^2 + bc = 1
for _ in range(50):
    a, b = rng.normal(size=2) + 1j * rng.normal(size=2); c = (1 - a**2) / b
    S = np.array([[a, b], [c, -a]]); J = 1j * S
    assert np.allclose(J @ J, -I2)
# Dimension of the conjugacy classes (rank of X -> [X, J])
def orbit_dim_real(J, m):
    cols = []
    for idx in range(m * m):
        X = np.zeros(m * m); X[idx] = 1; X = X.reshape(m, m)
        cols.append((X @ J - J @ X).ravel())
    return np.linalg.matrix_rank(np.array(cols).T)
for k in (1, 2, 3):                                                      # M_{2k}(R): dimension 2k^2
    J0 = np.kron(np.eye(k), np.array([[0., -1], [1, 0]]))
    assert orbit_dim_real(J0, 2 * k) == 2 * k * k
Jc = 1j * np.diag([1, -1]).astype(complex)                               # M2(C), class r = 1: real dimension 4
cols = []
for idx in range(4):
    for s in (1, 1j):
        X = np.zeros(4, dtype=complex); X[idx] = s; X = X.reshape(2, 2)
        cols.append(vec(X @ Jc - Jc @ X))
assert np.linalg.matrix_rank(np.array(cols).T) == 4

# ---------- III. Open systems ----------
m = 4
for _ in range(20):
    G = rng.normal(size=(m, m)); A = G - G.T
    R = rng.normal(size=(m, m)); D = R @ R.T + 0.1 * np.eye(m)            # positive-definite dissipation
    ev = np.linalg.eigvals(A - D)
    assert np.all(ev.real < 0)                                             # Re(lambda) = -z*Dz/|z|^2 < 0
    x0 = rng.normal(size=m)
    xt = expm(5.0 * (A - D)) @ x0
    assert np.linalg.norm(xt) < np.linalg.norm(x0) and np.linalg.norm(xt) > 0   # decays, never zero
# Energy balance on a forced solution: E(T) - E(0) = int (x.u - x.Dx)
G = rng.normal(size=(m, m)); A = G - G.T; R = rng.normal(size=(m, m)); D = 0.2 * R @ R.T
bvec = rng.normal(size=m); om_f = 1.3
u = lambda t: bvec * np.cos(om_f * t)
def rhs(t, z):
    x = z[:m]
    return np.r_[(A - D) @ x + u(t), [x @ u(t), x @ D @ x]]
sol = solve_ivp(rhs, (0, 200), np.r_[rng.normal(size=m), 0, 0], rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12)
x0, xT = sol.y[:m, 0], sol.y[:m, -1]
inj, dis = sol.y[m, -1], sol.y[m + 1, -1]
assert np.isclose(0.5 * xT @ xT - 0.5 * x0 @ x0, inj - dis, rtol=1e-6, atol=1e-6)
# Periodic regime: over one period, injected energy = dissipated energy > 0
Tper = 2 * np.pi / om_f
t0 = 200.0
sol2 = solve_ivp(rhs, (t0, t0 + Tper), np.r_[xT, 0, 0], rtol=1e-11, atol=1e-13)
assert np.isclose(sol2.y[m, -1], sol2.y[m + 1, -1], rtol=1e-4) and sol2.y[m + 1, -1] > 0
# Van der Pol: x'' - mu (1 - x^2) x' + x = 0; on the limit cycle, int mu (1 - x^2) x'^2 dt = 0
mu = 0.1
vdp = lambda t, z: [z[1], mu * (1 - z[0]**2) * z[1] - z[0], mu * (1 - z[0]**2) * z[1]**2]
s = solve_ivp(vdp, (0, 400), [0.5, 0, 0], rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12, dense_output=True)
tt = np.linspace(300, 400, 200001); X = s.sol(tt)
amp = X[0].max()
assert 1.95 < amp < 2.05                                                   # amplitude close to 2 for small mu
# balance over one period of the cycle (upward crossings of x = 0)
up = np.where((X[0][:-1] < 0) & (X[0][1:] >= 0))[0]
i0, i1 = up[-3], up[-2]
work = X[2][i1] - X[2][i0]
assert abs(work) < 1e-3 * abs(np.trapezoid(np.abs(mu * (1 - X[0][i0:i1]**2)) * X[1][i0:i1]**2, tt[i0:i1]))

Ar = np.zeros((4, 4)); Ar[0, 1], Ar[1, 0] = -1.0, 1.0; Ar[2, 3], Ar[3, 2] = -np.sqrt(2), np.sqrt(2)
best = min((np.linalg.norm(expm(t * Ar) - np.eye(4)), t) for t in np.arange(100, 3000, 0.05))
assert best[0] < 0.05
print("closest return after t = 100:", round(best[1], 2), "deviation", round(best[0], 4))
print("van der Pol cycle amplitude (mu = 0.1):", round(amp, 3))
# ---------- IV. Counterexamples and distinctions ----------
# M and -M define the same Möbius transformation
M = np.array([[0., -1.], [1., 0.]])
mobius = lambda B, z: (B[0, 0] * z + B[0, 1]) / (B[1, 0] * z + B[1, 1])

    assert np.isclose(mobius(M, z), mobius(-M, z)) and np.isclose(mobius(M, mobius(M, z)), z)
# Magnitude reversal away from zero, discontinuous extension that fixes zero
def f_disc(x):
    return np.zeros_like(x) if not np.any(x) else -x / (x @ x)
assert np.array_equal(f_disc(np.zeros(2)), np.zeros(2))
for x in (np.array([2., 0.]), np.array([1., 3.])):

# Without dissipation: persistent oscillation without input
